# How Chivabox Movers works

This page follows one lead from the quote form to the team's inbox. It then covers the data model, failure modes, scaling limits, security, and the design choices behind each part.

## Context: who uses it and where it runs

Chivabox Movers is a fictional moving company on Colombia's Caribbean coast. Its WordPress site embeds a `[moving_quote]` shortcode: a three-step form that prices a move in Colombian pesos (COP) from a move type, room count, origin and destination city, and optional extras, then saves the request as a lead.

The repo holds two parts:

- **The WordPress plugin** (`plugin/moving-quote/`): a pure pricing engine, the leads table and its REST API, the admin settings and leads pages, and signed delivery to n8n with a cron-driven retry outbox.
- **The n8n backend** (`workflows/`): three workflows that verify each signed lead, record it once, write it to a Google Sheet, and email the team and the customer.

The whole stack runs as one Docker Compose project, `chivabox-movers`:

- **WordPress 7.1.4** on PHP 8.3, published on `127.0.0.1:8080`, backed by MySQL 8.4 (unpublished)
- **A WP-CLI sidecar** (`cron`) that runs `wp cron event run --due-now` every 60 seconds, since WP-Cron itself is disabled in the container
- **n8n 2.45.6**, published on `127.0.0.1:5678`, backed by Postgres 17 (unpublished)
- **Redis and `n8n-worker`** (optional, `compose.queue.yaml`): queue mode, covered under Scaling

WordPress reaches n8n only over the Compose network, at `http://n8n:5678`; nothing outside the stack calls n8n. The backend calls two external services under one Google account: Google Sheets and Gmail.

## How a lead moves from the form to the inbox

The sequence diagram groups the path into six segments. Open the [interactive page](diagrams/lead-sequence.html) to read it full-size.

![Lead delivery sequence: estimate, submit and insert, signed POST and verify, record and respond, hand off to process-lead, and an alternative retry loop when delivery first fails](diagrams/lead-sequence.svg)

Three things the diagram's boxes and arrows can't show on their own:

- **The insert sets a lease, not just a row.** `insert()` writes `next_attempt_at = now + LEASE_SECONDS` (120 s) before the inline delivery attempt runs, so no cron pass can claim that same row as due until the lease expires — see "Concurrent inline and cron sends" below.
- **WordPress prices the move itself.** `LeadController` ignores any `amount`/`low`/`high` the client sent and recomputes the estimate from the current settings before saving, so a tampered client-side price never reaches the stored lead or the sheet.
- **The 5-second ceiling is a blocking one.** The inline delivery attempt runs synchronously inside the same request that answers the browser, so a slow (not just a failed) n8n response can delay the customer's `201` by up to `WebhookClient`'s 5-second timeout before WordPress gives up and answers anyway.

## Data model

| Store | What it holds |
|---|---|
| **The leads table** (`{$wpdb->prefix}moving_quote_leads`, MySQL) | One row per `request_uuid` (unique key): the move's fields, the computed estimate, the lifecycle `status` (`new`/`contacted`/`quoted`/`won`/`lost`), and the delivery state (`sync_status`, `attempts`, `next_attempt_at`, `last_error`, `sent_at`). Indexed on `(status, created_at)` for the admin list and `(sync_status, next_attempt_at)` for the outbox's due-row query. |
| **`processed_events`** (Postgres, database `app`) | `(source, event_id)` as the primary key, `received_at` as a timestamp. `lead-intake`'s idempotency gate: `source` is always `'wordpress-lead'`, `event_id` is the lead's `request_uuid`. |
| **The settings option** (`moving_quote_settings`, autoloaded) | One array: base rates, per-room and per-km prices, extras prices, the range percentage, the routes list, and the three delivery fields (`webhook_url`, `webhook_secret`, `team_email`), each `''` until the owner sets it. |
| **The sheet** (`Moving leads` tab, external) | One row per accepted lead, 15 columns in a fixed header order, written in `RAW` cell format so a phone number or a formula-looking name is stored exactly as sent. |

## Failure modes

Unless a row says otherwise, every Postgres, Google Sheets, and Gmail node retries 3 times, 1,000 ms apart. A failure that outlasts its retries fails the execution, and `error-handler` emails the operator. Delivery backs off `60 × 2^(attempts-1)` seconds after each failed attempt, doubling up to a 6-hour cap, and gives up after 10 attempts — about 8.5 hours after the first failure (`SyncTransition.php:21-22`). A `failed` lead only moves again when an admin clicks "Retry delivery" (`LeadRepository::requeue()`), which resets it to `pending` with 0 attempts, due immediately.

| Failure | Behavior | Detection | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| n8n is down when WordPress delivers | `WebhookClient::send()`'s `wp_remote_post()` fails with a transport error. The lead stays `pending`, backed off per `Backoff::delay_after()`. | The admin's Delivery column, or the stuck-delivery notice | Bring n8n back up. The outbox's cron-driven retry (the sidecar's 60 s loop) resends the same signed lead once its backoff elapses; n8n dedupes on `jti`. If backoff has already pushed the lead to `failed`, use "Retry delivery" instead of waiting. |
| n8n is slow (past the 5 s timeout) | `WebhookClient`'s 5 s timeout fires before n8n answers. WordPress records the timeout as `last_error` and backs off, but the request may still complete on n8n's side — so the retry's own signed POST reaches `lead-intake` as a second, otherwise-identical request for the same `jti`, which n8n answers `duplicate`. | Nothing distinguishes this from an ordinary retry after the fact: a successful attempt clears `last_error` to `''` (`SyncTransition.php:16`), and WordPress keeps no history of which answer (`accepted` or `duplicate`) a past attempt got. The only trace is a lead that ends `sent` with 2 or more attempts. | None needed: `jti` dedupe means the retry never double-records the request, even though it reached n8n twice. |
| A wrong or rotated webhook secret | Every signed request fails `lead-intake`'s Webhook node's own `jwtAuth` check (plain-text 403, bad signature) before any execution starts. Then use "Retry delivery" on any lead already `failed`, or wait for the next outbox pass on one still `pending`. |

The webhook call never reaches a workflow execution: n8n's own middleware answers the POST with `503 {"code":503,"message":"Database is not ready!"}` before any node runs. WordPress recorded that as the lead's `last_error` (`HTTP 503: {"code":503,"message":"Database is not ready!"}`) and left it `pending`. | `docker compose ps` showing `postgres` unhealthy, or every delivery attempt's `last_error` reading the same 503 message. | Restore Postgres (`docker compose start postgres`). No alert went out during the outage — the request never started an execution, so `error-handler` never ran. |
| Config missing or unreadable (`config/config.json`) | Read/Write Files v1.1 throws its own `No file(s)…` error directly when `fileSelector` matches nothing, failing the whole execution before "Record request" ever runs — n8n answers its own 5xx, not a silent 200. The outbox's next retry (or "Retry delivery" on a `failed` lead) resends it. |
| n8n rejects the payload (422 `invalid_payload`) | `src/nodes/lead.ts`'s `parseLead()` rejects the body's `estimate` shape. WordPress sees a non-2xx and backs off like any other delivery failure; the lead stays `pending`, then eventually `failed` after about 8.5 hours, with no operator alert — `Verify request` answering 422 fails no execution and has no `errorWorkflow` to trigger. | The stuck-delivery admin notice, or the lead's own `last_error` (`HTTP 422: {...}`) | Fix whatever made the body fail parsing, then Retry delivery on the `failed` lead. The parity corpus (`tests/fixtures/lead-payloads/*.json`, `tests/php/Delivery/LeadPayloadParityTest.php`, `tests/nodes/lead-payload-parity.test.ts`) pins the shapes in that corpus as accepted on the TypeScript side, so a drift between the two there is caught before it ships rather than discovered as a stuck lead. |
| Sheets or Gmail fails | "Append lead row", "Email team", and "Email customer" each retry 3 times, 1,000 ms apart. Past that, the execution fails and `error-handler` emails the operator naming the failing node. A retry from n8n's executions list resumes at the failed node and reuses every node's output before it. | The `error-handler` email | Fix the cause (quota, credential, renamed tab), then retry the failed `process-lead` execution from n8n's UI. |

The result is a second sheet row for the one lead, or a second copy of the team or customer email — not a second lead in WordPress, which only ever calls `process-lead` once per accepted lead. | A second `Moving leads` row, or a duplicate email, for one `reference` | An operator retry always resumes at the node that failed, not from the top, so only that one node's own effect can double — never the whole execution. | WP-Cron (the sidecar) isn't running | The outbox's cron-driven retries stop; leads inserted while delivery was already failing stay `pending` indefinitely past their backoff. | MySQL is down | WordPress can't boot: every page, including the quote form and the REST API, fails at the database layer. | `docker compose ps` showing `db` unhealthy, or every page failing to load | Restore MySQL (`docker compose up -d --wait db`). The submission was never saved, so the visitor has to resubmit once the site is back. |
| A stale nonce from a page cache | A cached page serves an old `X-WP-Nonce`. WordPress core's own `rest_cookie_check_errors` rejects it before the plugin's route ever runs: 403 `rest_cookie_invalid_nonce`. | The form's own 403 message ("Your session expired. Reload the page and try again.") | Reloading only helps if the reload itself bypasses the cache; while a page cache keeps serving the same stale page, the nonce stays stale too. ## Scaling

- **Outbox batch and real cron.** `Outbox::process_due()` claims up to `BATCH_SIZE` (20) due rows per pass. The cron sidecar runs every 60 seconds; a real deployment points system cron at the same `wp cron event run` command instead of a container loop. The consequence of the batch size: the outbox can drain a backlog of failed leads at most 20 a minute, so a long outage followed by recovery takes proportionally longer to clear than it took to build up.
- **Queue mode.** `compose.queue.yaml` adds Redis 8.8.4 and `n8n-worker` containers, which run executions instead of the main process. `docker compose -f compose.yaml -f compose.queue.yaml up -d --wait --scale n8n-worker=2` brought Redis, n8n, and two workers up healthy, with the intake suite passing 13/13 against them, including the five-concurrent-deliveries case giving exactly one `accepted`. The override gives Redis no password; set `QUEUE_BULL_REDIS_PASSWORD` before production use.
- **The Sheets write quota.** Google's documented limits: 60 read requests and 60 write requests per minute per user per project, and 300 of each per minute per project ([Sheets API usage limits](https://developers.google.com/workspace/sheets/api/limits), checked 2026-11-02). "Append lead row" makes one write per accepted lead, so a single demo account tops out near 60 leads a minute on that quota before Google answers 429, which the node's 3 retries 1,000 ms apart can't outlast.
- **Gmail sending limits.** The demo uses a personal Gmail account, not a Google Workspace account; Workspace accounts have different limits ([Gmail sending limits](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/22839), checked 2026-11-02). For a personal account, the page documents two separate thresholds that trigger "You have reached a limit for sending mail": "a total of more than 500 recipients in a single email," and, separately, "more than 500 emails sent in a day" — after either, sending typically resumes within 1 to 24 hours. Every email here has exactly one recipient, so the per-email recipient limit never applies; the one that binds is the daily-count limit. Each accepted lead sends 2 emails (team, customer), so that limit caps the account near 250 leads a day.

## Security

- **Delivery.** JWT HS256, with the algorithm pinned by the `jwtAuth` credential (not read from the token's own header), a 300-second lifetime, and a `body_sha256` claim binding the signature to the exact bytes sent. `jti` (= `request_uuid`) is the one mechanism that both dedupes a replay in n8n and serves as the idempotency key in `processed_events` — the two are the same check, not two separate ones.
- **Secret storage.** The webhook secret lives in `.env` (`LEAD_WEBHOOK_SECRET`, gitignored) and the WordPress `moving_quote_settings` option today. NOTE: a production deployment should move it to a `wp-config.php` constant instead (e.g. `define( 'MOVING_QUOTE_WEBHOOK_SECRET', '…' )`, read in place of the option field), so the secret never sits in the database at all — the upgrade path this demo doesn't take.
- **Request checks.** Every visitor's `POST /leads` request is checked against the REST `wp_rest` nonce (`X-WP-Nonce`), every admin handler checks `manage_options` before its own nonce, every hand-written SQL query goes through `$wpdb->prepare()` with `%i` for the table name (the install schema uses `dbDelta`, and a few lifecycle writes use `$wpdb->insert()`/`$wpdb->update()` instead, which escape their values without a separate `prepare()` call), a honeypot field (`website`) catches a bot that fills every field by DOM access, and a fixed-window rate limit (120/600 s for estimates, 5/3,600 s for submissions by default, both filterable through `moving_quote_rate_limits`) is keyed on a hashed client IP under a MySQL named lock, so a concurrent burst can't all read the same stale count. `RateLimiter::client_ip()` reads `REMOTE_ADDR`, filterable through `moving_quote_client_ip` for a site behind a trusted reverse proxy (see AGENTS.md for the trusted-proxy example); every `submit` 429 also increments a counter for a fixed 24-hour window (no IP, no PII) that `StuckDeliveryNotice` surfaces once it's non-zero, so a burst of rate-limited customers is visible to the site owner instead of silent.
- **Spam bound: the customer auto-reply can relay to any address.** The customer email (see "process-lead" in AGENTS.md) is sent to whatever address the form's `email` field holds — there's no verification that the submitter owns it — and its body echoes the submitted `name`. A submitter could use the form to relay a bounded amount of unsolicited mail to a third party's address, with their own chosen name in the body. What bounds it: the honeypot (a bot filling every field, including `website`, is turned away before anything is saved or sent), the `submit` rate limit (5 per hour per IP), and Gmail's own daily sending cap (documented in "Scaling" above). A CAPTCHA on the form is the upgrade path once that bound isn't enough for a given deployment's traffic; this demo doesn't add one.
- **Output safety.** CSV cells pass through `Admin\CsvEscaper`, which prefixes a value starting with `=`, `+`, `-`, `@`, a tab, or a carriage return with `'`, so a formula-looking name can never execute when the export opens in a spreadsheet application. The Google Sheets append writes cells in `RAW` format, so neither a phone number nor a formula-looking name is reinterpreted by the sheet's own type coercion. The team's plain-text email has no markup to break out of, but it does have lines to forge: a control character in a lead field could otherwise inject an extra "Phone:"/"Email:" line into the alert. `LeadValidator::validate_name()` (WordPress) normalizes every C0 control, DEL, and Unicode line/paragraph separator in `customer.name` to a single space before a lead is ever saved — it normalizes the name only. `move.origin` and `move.destination` need no normalization of their own: `validate_city()` only accepts a value matching one of the merchant's own configured city names, so neither field can ever carry a control character a configured city name doesn't already have. `map-lead.ts`'s `oneLine()` still runs the same normalization on every lead-derived value (name and cities alike) interpolated into the team email's body, as a second check on the n8n side, independent of what WordPress already guarantees. The email address rule on both sides (`LeadValidator::validate_email()`, `src/nodes/lead.ts`) rejects any address containing whitespace, a control character, or any of `" , ; < > ( ) [ ] : \`, which closes the one gap `FILTER_VALIDATE_EMAIL` alone leaves open — a quoted local part such as `"a,b"@example.com`, which Gmail's own `sendTo` would otherwise split into a second recipient. Every dynamic value in the customer's HTML email is escaped separately, since that email is HTML, not plain text.
- **Data protection.** No IP address is ever stored: `RateLimiter`'s bucket key is `hash_hmac('sha256', ip, wp_salt('nonce'))`, truncated to 20 hex characters. The Postgres role for the app database (`APP_DB_USER`) can't reach the `n8n` database and vice versa (`REVOKE ALL … FROM PUBLIC`). Every long-running service's port that doesn't need to be reachable from outside Docker (MySQL, Postgres) is never published; only WordPress (`8080`) and n8n (`5678`) are, both on `127.0.0.1`. See "Token retention, precisely" below for what n8n itself keeps and for how long.

### Token retention, precisely

n8n 2.45.6 stores the incoming `Authorization` header **unredacted** with every run that passes the Webhook node's own `jwtAuth` check: the node builds its output from the raw `req.headers`, so the token lands in `execution_data` from the start, for every outcome — accepted, duplicate, or rejected.

- **Successful runs are soft-deleted, not kept.** Both `lead-intake.json` and `process-lead.json` set `saveDataSuccessExecution: 'none'` in their own workflow settings (`error-handler` doesn't set it). A successful run is soft-deleted with a backdated `deletedAt` instead of kept, then purged at n8n's next hard-delete cycle — about 15 minutes by default, longer after a restart, since each restart resets that cycle's own timer.
- **Failed runs** keep the token and the customer's name, phone, and email for the full `EXECUTIONS_DATA_MAX_AGE`, 168 hours (7 days) in this stack.
- **Manual runs** (started from n8n's own editor) are purged under `saveManualExecutions: false` — also set in both `lead-intake.json` and `process-lead.json` — without the backdate applied to automatic runs, so they're purged an hour or more later than a production run.
- **What bounds the exposure in the meantime:** the token's own 300-second lifetime (checked independently of whatever n8n stores), its `body_sha256` binding to one exact request body, and its `jti` — the lead's own `request_uuid` — which `processed_events`' primary key dedupes. A token read out of a stored execution, even before it's purged, can replay the same lead but can never create a second one. There is no execution-level redaction in n8n 2.45.6 for a `responseNode` webhook; the mitigation here is a bounded blast radius and a bounded window, not a hidden token.

## Decisions

- **Testing strategy.** PHPUnit 11 covers every pure class with exact cases and no WordPress bootstrap; a stack suite built on Vitest drives the real Compose stack over HTTP and WP-CLI for everything WordPress-coupled. The n8n half of the stack suite needs Postgres and n8n running too, so it runs locally rather than in CI's `stack` job. WordPress's own core test suite was rejected: WordPress 7.1.4's suite still needs `yoast/phpunit-polyfills ^1.1`, which caps PHPUnit at 9, below this project's PHPUnit 11 standard. `wp-env` was rejected too — it would boot a second WordPress next to the Compose stack the README already runs. See `docs/testing.md` for the full breakdown.
- **JWT over a hand-rolled HMAC header.** n8n 2.45.6's Webhook node natively supports `jwtAuth`, checked against the real instance's source (`Webhook/utils.ts`). Using it keeps the secret in an n8n credential rather than a workflow expression, and it rejects a missing or badly signed token before any execution starts. A hand-rolled HMAC header would need the same signing work on the WordPress side, plus a Code node on the n8n side to parse and verify it — strictly more code for the same guarantee.
- **One UUID end to end.** The browser mints `submission_id` once per page load; it becomes the leads table's `request_uuid`, the JWT's `jti`, and `processed_events`' `event_id`. One identifier threading through every layer means a retry at any point — a double submit, a cron retry, an n8n-side replay — is recognizable as "the same request" without translating between separate ID schemes.
- **Inline first attempt plus the outbox.** A lead's first delivery attempt runs synchronously, right after the insert, so a lead delivered on the first try reaches n8n within the same request-response cycle — no 60-second wait for the next cron tick. The outbox then exists for every attempt after that: it's the only path for a lead whose inline attempt failed, and it's what makes delivery eventually consistent instead of best-effort-once.
- **The cron sidecar.** WP-Cron's own request-triggered pseudo-cron only runs when a visitor loads a page, which can't be relied on for a low-traffic demo site to retry a failed delivery promptly. A dedicated sidecar running `wp cron event run --due-now` on a fixed 60-second loop makes the retry schedule actually run on schedule, standing in for the system cron a real deployment would configure instead.
- **A committed autoloader and assets.** `plugin/moving-quote/vendor/` (the Composer autoloader) and `plugin/moving-quote/assets/quote-form.js` (the esbuild bundle) are committed rather than built at deploy time, because the plugin ships as a folder a WordPress site installs directly — there's no build step on the receiving end. CI fails when either drifts from its source, so the committed copy can never go stale silently.
- **Team email in WordPress settings.** The team's notification address (`team_email`) is set once in the WordPress admin, not hardcoded in n8n's `config/config.json`, and travels inside the signed payload on every lead. A site that changes its team inbox updates one WordPress setting, with no n8n redeploy.
- **Postgres idempotency.** `processed_events`' primary key `(source, event_id)` is the one mechanism that makes `lead-intake`'s "Record request" step atomic: the insert and the duplicate check are the same statement (`INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING RETURNING event_id`), so two concurrent deliveries of the same lead can't both read "not yet recorded" and both proceed — exactly one gets a real `event_id` back.
- **REST endpoints register with no `args` schema.** A restrictive schema would make WordPress core answer its own 400 `rest_invalid_param` before the route ever runs, preempting the plugin's documented 422 contract. Every field is validated by `LeadValidator` instead, so every rejection reason is one the API contract actually documents.
- **The `submit` rate limit counts only a lead about to be inserted.** Counting it earlier (on any POST to `/leads`) would burn through a customer's 5-per-hour budget on a typo they're correcting, or a double-tap on Submit — attempts that were never going to save anything. Counting it after the honeypot, validation, and the duplicate lookup all pass means only a genuinely new lead spends that budget.
- **Append-before-email in `process-lead`.** The sheet row writes before either notification email, so the order guarantees only one direction: no email ever goes out for a lead that isn't in the sheet. It does not guarantee the reverse — a Gmail failure after a successful append still leaves that row in the sheet with no matching email sent, until someone retries the execution. The cost, documented as a known gap above, is that a failed append itself silently skips both emails too.
- **CSV cells guarded against formula injection, not merely escaped for CSV syntax.** `Admin\CsvEscaper` targets the specific prefix set (`=`, `+`, `-`, `@`, tab, carriage return) that makes a spreadsheet application — not just a CSV parser — read a cell as a formula, since the export's whole purpose is to be opened in one.
- **A stuck-delivery admin notice, not a silent retry queue.** A `failed` lead, or one `pending` for over an hour, is surfaced directly on the Dashboard and the Leads page to a `manage_options` user, rather than relying on the outbox's own backoff schedule (up to 6 hours between attempts) to eventually resolve or exhaust it unnoticed. A lead the team has already closed out (`won` or `lost`) is excluded from the count: once a human has resolved the lead, its own delivery status stops being something the owner needs to act on.
